Polynomials

The CKKS scheme works over polynomials in rings of the form $\bigl(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\bigr)[X]/(X^N + 1)$. To make everything concrete, we'll pin down specific numbers throughout this part. Let $N = 2^{16} = 65536$ be the ring dimension, and we fix a chain of distinct primes $q_0, q_1, \ldots, q_{15}$, all congruent to $1$ modulo $2^{17} = 131072$, with $q_0 \approx 2^{55}$ and $q_1 \approx q_2 \approx \cdots \approx q_{15} \approx 2^{40}$. Throughout, we'll write

$$ R_\infty = \mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^{65536} + 1), \qquad R_\ell = \bigl(\mathbb{Z}/(q_0 q_1 \cdots q_\ell)\mathbb{Z}\bigr)[X]/(X^{65536} + 1), $$

so $R_\ell$ is the polynomial ring at level $\ell \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 15\}$, and $R_\infty$ is the underlying integer-coefficient ring.

Everything in CKKS is built on top of two representations of the polynomials in $R_\ell$ (polynomials in $R_\infty$ are stored as polynomials in $R_{15}$) and ten operations on them, each introduced in its own subsection below. Seven of the operations keep the polynomial in the same ring; two drop it to a lower-level ring; and one raises it to a higher-level ring.

Representing integers modulo $q_0 \cdots q_\ell$

Both representations of polynomials in $R_\ell$ ultimately store integers modulo $q = q_0 \cdots q_\ell$, so we start there. An integer modulo $q$ is stored in residue number system (RNS) form: the RNS representation of $r \in \mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$ is the tuple

$$ (r_0, r_1, \ldots, r_\ell) \in \mathbb{Z}^{\ell+1} \quad\text{with}\quad 0 \le r_i < q_i \;\text{ and }\; r_i \equiv r \pmod{q_i} \;\text{ for each } i. $$

This tuple exists and is unique by the Chinese remainder theorem. The reason for using RNS form is twofold:

  • Arithmetic is pointwise. Adding two integers modulo $q$ in RNS form is just componentwise addition modulo each $q_i$, and likewise for multiplication. No big-integer arithmetic is needed.
  • Each $q_i$ fits in a machine word. Every $q_i$ is at most $2^{55}$, so each component $r_i$ fits in a single $64$-bit register, and the componentwise operations map directly onto fast hardware instructions.

Representing polynomials in $R_\ell$

A polynomial in $R_\ell$ has two equivalent representations, the coefficient domain and the evaluation domain. Both store the polynomial as a tuple of $65536$ integers modulo $q = q_0 \cdots q_\ell$, each in RNS form; the two representations differ in what those integers mean.

Coefficient domain. Lift $p(X) \in R_\ell$ to its unique representative $c_0 + c_1 X + \cdots + c_{65535} X^{65535}$ in $\bigl(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\bigr)[X]$ of degree at most $65535$. The coefficient form of $p(X)$ is the tuple $(c_0, c_1, \ldots, c_{65535})$, with each $c_j \in \mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$ stored in RNS form.

Evaluation domain. Let $\zeta \in \mathbb{Z}/(q_0 \cdots q_{15})\mathbb{Z}$ be a primitive $131072$-th root of unity — that is, a root of $X^{65536} + 1$ — fixed once and for all. The evaluation form of $p(X)$ is the tuple

$$ \bigl(p(\zeta),\; p(\zeta^3),\; p(\zeta^5),\; \ldots,\; p(\zeta^{131071})\bigr), $$

with each $p(\zeta^{2k+1}) \in \mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$ stored in RNS form. The evaluation $p(\zeta^{2k+1})$ is well-defined because $\zeta^{2k+1}$ is itself a root of $X^{65536} + 1$, so the value doesn't depend on which representative of $p(X)$ in $\bigl(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\bigr)[X]$ we plug in.

Number-theoretic transform

The number-theoretic transform (NTT) converts a polynomial from coefficient form to evaluation form, and its inverse (iNTT) converts back. From the definition of evaluation form, the two are related by the matrix equation

$$ \begin{pmatrix} p(\zeta) \\ p(\zeta^3) \\ p(\zeta^5) \\ \vdots \\ p(\zeta^{131071}) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \zeta & \zeta^{2} & \cdots & \zeta^{65535} \\ 1 & \zeta^{3} & \zeta^{6} & \cdots & \zeta^{3 \cdot 65535} \\ 1 & \zeta^{5} & \zeta^{10} & \cdots & \zeta^{5 \cdot 65535} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & \zeta^{131071} & \zeta^{2 \cdot 131071} & \cdots & \zeta^{65535 \cdot 131071} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_{65535} \end{pmatrix} \pmod{q_0 \cdots q_\ell}. $$

The $(k, j)$ entry of the matrix is $\zeta^{(2k+1) j}$. This transformation is the negacyclic number-theoretic transform; its inverse (the matrix that converts evaluation form back to coefficient form) is the inverse negacyclic number-theoretic transform.

The NTT is, in essence, a discrete Fourier transform over a finite ring rather than over $\mathbb{C}$. The "negacyclic" qualifier is because the usual DFT is built from the roots of $X^N - 1$, whereas here we use the roots of $X^N + 1$. The twisted exponents $\{1, 3, 5, \ldots, 2N - 1\}$ in the matrix above are precisely the exponents that make $\zeta^{2k+1}$ a root of $X^N + 1$.

Addition and subtraction

Adding or subtracting two polynomials in $R_\ell$ is componentwise, provided both inputs are in the same representation. If $(a_0, \ldots, a_{65535})$ and $(b_0, \ldots, b_{65535})$ are the tuples (in either coefficient or evaluation form), the sum and difference are

$$ (a_0 \pm b_0,\; a_1 \pm b_1,\; \ldots,\; a_{65535} \pm b_{65535}) \pmod{q_0 \cdots q_\ell}, $$

with each modular operation carried out componentwise in RNS form. Mixing representations is not allowed: one input must be converted to match the other via NTT or iNTT first.

Scalar multiplication

Multiplying a polynomial $p(X) \in R_\ell$ by a scalar $c \in \mathbb{Z}/(q_0 \cdots q_\ell)\mathbb{Z}$ is componentwise multiplication by $c$ in either representation: $(a_0, \ldots, a_{65535}) \mapsto (c\, a_0, \ldots, c\, a_{65535})$.

Polynomial multiplication

Multiplying two polynomials in $R_\ell$ requires both inputs to be in evaluation form. The product is then componentwise:

$$ \bigl(a(\zeta),\, a(\zeta^3),\, \ldots\bigr) \cdot \bigl(b(\zeta),\, b(\zeta^3),\, \ldots\bigr) = \bigl(a(\zeta)\, b(\zeta),\, a(\zeta^3)\, b(\zeta^3),\, \ldots\bigr), $$

which is the evaluation form of $a(X)\, b(X)$ because $a(\zeta^{2k+1})\, b(\zeta^{2k+1}) = (ab)(\zeta^{2k+1})$ at every root. In coefficient form, the same operation would be a convolution, costing $O(N^2)$ rather than $O(N)$ — this is the entire reason evaluation form exists.

If either input is in coefficient form, it must be converted to evaluation form via NTT first; the cost of that conversion is what makes multiplication asymptotically more expensive than addition.

Automorphism

For each odd integer $i$, the map

$$ \tau_i : R_\ell \longrightarrow R_\ell, \qquad p(X) \longmapsto p(X^i) $$

is a ring automorphism of $R_\ell$ — hence the name. (It's only required that $i$ be odd modulo $2N = 131072$, since $\tau_i = \tau_{i + 2N}$.) The automorphism by $i$ can be computed efficiently in either representation.

In coefficient form. Using $X^{65536} = -1$, the image of $X^j$ under $\tau_i$ is $X^{ij} = (-1)^{\lfloor ij / 65536 \rfloor}\, X^{ij \bmod 65536}$, so

$$ \tau_i\!\left(\sum_{j=0}^{65535} c_j X^j\right) = \sum_{j=0}^{65535} (-1)^{\lfloor ij/65536 \rfloor}\, c_j\, X^{ij \bmod 65536}. $$

Concretely: the new coefficient at position $ij \bmod 65536$ is $\pm c_j$, with the sign determined by $\lfloor ij / 65536 \rfloor$. Since $i$ is odd, the map $j \mapsto ij \bmod 65536$ is a permutation, so applying $\tau_i$ amounts to permuting the coefficient tuple and flipping the signs of some of its entries.

In evaluation form. The $k$-th component of $\tau_i(p)$ is

$$ \tau_i(p)(\zeta^{2k+1}) = p\bigl(\zeta^{i(2k+1)}\bigr) = p\bigl(\zeta^{2k'+1}\bigr), $$

where $k'$ is the unique index in $\{0, 1, \ldots, 65535\}$ with $2k' + 1 \equiv i(2k + 1) \pmod{131072}$. So automorphism in evaluation form is just a permutation of the tuple — no sign flips at all.

Modulus reduction

Given a target level $\ell' < \ell$, the modulus reduction operation is the natural ring homomorphism

$$ R_\ell \longrightarrow R_{\ell'} $$

induced by reducing every coefficient modulo $q_0 \cdots q_{\ell'}$. In RNS form, this is essentially free: every integer modulo $q_0 \cdots q_\ell$ is already stored as the tuple $(r_0, r_1, \ldots, r_\ell)$, and its image modulo $q_0 \cdots q_{\ell'}$ is just the prefix $(r_0, r_1, \ldots, r_{\ell'})$. Forget the higher-prime components and the work is done. This applies in both coefficient and evaluation form.

Modulus raise

Modulus raise must be performed in coefficient form.

Given a target level $\ell' > \ell$, the modulus raise operation maps $R_\ell \to R_{\ell'}$ by lifting each coefficient to its centered integer representative and then reinterpreting modulo the larger product. Concretely, a polynomial $c_0 + c_1 X + \cdots + c_{65535} X^{65535}$ in $R_\ell$ with each $c_j$ lifted to the symmetric interval $\bigl[-\tfrac{1}{2} q_0 \cdots q_\ell,\, \tfrac{1}{2} q_0 \cdots q_\ell\bigr)$ maps to the same polynomial, now viewed in $R_{\ell'}$. This map is not a ring homomorphism — there's no algebraic relationship between $R_\ell$ and $R_{\ell'}$ being preserved — but it's exactly the right operation for bootstrapping (see Section 3.4).

To compute modulus raise efficiently in RNS, work one coefficient at a time. Suppose $r$ is an integer modulo $q = q_0 \cdots q_\ell$ stored as $(r_0, r_1, \ldots, r_\ell)$, and we want its residues $r \bmod q_j$ for each $j \in \{\ell + 1, \ldots, \ell'\}$. Precompute the modular inverses

$$ w_i = \bigl(q / q_i\bigr)^{-1} \bmod q_i, \qquad i \in \{0, 1, \ldots, \ell\}. $$

Then, given $(r_0, \ldots, r_\ell)$, compute

$$ t_i = w_i\, r_i \bmod q_i \in [0,\, q_i), \qquad v = \mathrm{round}\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\ell} \frac{t_i}{q_i}\right) \in \mathbb{Z}. $$

By the Chinese remainder theorem, $r \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{\ell} t_i\, (q / q_i) \pmod{q}$, so the centered representative of $r$ is $\sum_i t_i\, (q / q_i) - v\, q$. Therefore, for each new prime $q_j$ with $j > \ell$,

$$ r \bmod q_j \;=\; \left[\sum_{i=0}^{\ell} t_i\, (q / q_i) \;-\; v\, q\right] \bmod q_j. $$

The inner sum and $v$ are computed once per coefficient and reused across all the new primes $q_{\ell + 1}, \ldots, q_{\ell'}$.

Exercise: verify that the $v$ defined above really does round $\sum_i t_i\, (q / q_i)$ to the nearest multiple of $q$, so that $\sum_i t_i\, (q / q_i) - v\, q$ lands in $\bigl[-q/2,\, q/2\bigr)$.

Rescale

Rescale must be performed in coefficient form.

Given a target level $\ell' < \ell$, the rescale operation maps $R_\ell \to R_{\ell'}$ by dividing every coefficient by $Q'' = q_{\ell'+1} \cdots q_\ell$ and rounding to the nearest integer:

$$ c_0 + c_1 X + \cdots + c_{65535} X^{65535} \;\longmapsto\; \mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{c_0}{Q''}\right) + \mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{c_1}{Q''}\right) X + \cdots + \mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{c_{65535}}{Q''}\right) X^{65535}, $$

where each $c_j$ is first lifted to its centered representative in $\bigl[-\tfrac{1}{2} q_0 \cdots q_\ell,\, \tfrac{1}{2} q_0 \cdots q_\ell\bigr)$. Like modulus raise, rescale is not a ring homomorphism, but the rounding error on each coefficient is bounded by $\ell - \ell'$ — one half-unit per dropped prime — and rescale satisfies

$$ \mathrm{rescale}(a(X) + b(X)) \approx \mathrm{rescale}(a(X)) + \mathrm{rescale}(b(X)) $$

up to that small error. This near-linearity is what lets rescale be used inside ciphertext operations without disrupting their additive structure.

To compute rescale in RNS, observe that $\mathrm{round}(c_j / Q'') = (c_j - \mathrm{centered}(c_j \bmod Q'')) / Q''$, where the numerator is an exact multiple of $Q''$. Precompute the modular inverses

$$ w_i = (Q'' / q_i)^{-1} \bmod q_i, \qquad i \in \{\ell' + 1, \ldots, \ell\} $$

(note these are different $w_i$ from the modulus-raise version — the product is $Q''$, not $q$). Then, given the RNS components $r_{\ell' + 1}, \ldots, r_\ell$ of a coefficient $c_j$, set

$$ t_i = w_i\, r_i \bmod q_i, \qquad s = \left[\sum_{i = \ell' + 1}^{\ell} t_i\, (Q'' / q_i)\right] \bmod Q'', $$

and let $\tilde{s}$ be the centered version of $s$ in $[-Q''/2,\, Q''/2)$. The rescaled coefficient, modulo $q_j$ for any $j \le \ell'$, is then

$$ \mathrm{rescale}(c_j) \bmod q_j \;=\; (r_j - \tilde{s})\, (Q'')^{-1} \bmod q_j, $$

where $(Q'')^{-1} \bmod q_j$ is also precomputed. The subtraction inside the parentheses is divisible by $Q''$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, so this division is exact modulo every $q_j$ for $j \le \ell'$.
